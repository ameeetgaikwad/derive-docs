## Why BNB Chain L1

Hedge deploys directly on BNB Chain (BSC) L1 — not on its own app-chain, and not on an L2. The reasoning:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Fast, cheap blockspace" icon="zap">
    Post-Fermi (January 2026), BSC runs ~0.45 s blocks with ~1.1 s finality at ~0.05 gwei gas. An RFQ protocol — two signatures and one transaction per trade — fits comfortably; there is no need for a dedicated sequencer.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Liquidity where BTC lives" icon="droplets">
    BTCB and deep stablecoin liquidity (USDT-dominant) are native to BSC, alongside the oracle and market-maker hedging infrastructure an options protocol depends on. No bridge friction for users or makers.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Gasless UX path" icon="fuel">
    BNB Chain's **MegaFuel paymaster** sponsors plain-EOA transactions (no account-abstraction migration), and **EIP-7702** (live since the Pascal upgrade) enables batched approve-and-trade. Deposits, trades, and settlement can be sponsored without rebuilding wallets.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Options are white space" icon="telescope">
    BNB Chain has enormous spot and perp volume but no major live options venue. A BTC-yield product on the chain where retail BTCB actually sits is distribution, not just deployment.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

### Why not an app-chain or L2?

Protocols that run their own chain typically do so because portfolio-margin risk checks burn tens of millions of gas per trade, forcing a private sequencer. Hedge v1 uses standard margin with an RFQ execution path — cheap enough for shared blockspace. Running an app-chain would have meant sequencer operations, bridge liquidity bootstrapping, and onboarding friction for market makers, while forfeiting BNB-native distribution. The chain question gets revisited only if the protocol evolves into sub-100 ms cross-margined order-book territory.

Settlement-critical transactions are submitted by the protocol's executor; private transaction relays are used to keep executor flow out of the public mempool.

## Licensing and provenance

The on-chain contracts are a **fork of audited, open-source options-protocol contracts**, used strictly under their open-source licenses:

| Layer | License | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Core protocol (ledger, assets, risk manager, feeds) | **GPL-3.0-or-later** | Forked from a pinned snapshot whose BUSL-1.1 change date has passed, converting it to GPL-3.0. Later upstream commits remain under BUSL and are **not** used. |
| Matching layer (Matching, ActionVerifier, RfqModule, deposit/withdraw/transfer modules) | **AGPL-3.0** | Forked under AGPL. Modifications are published, as the license requires — the contracts are verified on-chain in any case. |
| Math utility libraries | **AGPL-3.0** | Forked. |

What this means in practice:

- **Audited foundation.** The base contracts were audited by **Sigma Prime** (reports are public). Hedge pins the audited snapshot, keeps the vendored source read-only, and limits its own surface to deployment scripts, configuration, and the off-chain services. Changes relative to the base are the natural target for differential audits.
- **Full rebrand, no trademarks.** GPL and AGPL grant code rights, not trademark rights. Hedge is an independent deployment with its own name, domain separator, addresses, and operations — it has no affiliation with, or endorsement from, the upstream protocol's authors.
- **Copyleft honored.** Modifications to AGPL-licensed code are published. On-chain bytecode is source-verified on BscScan.
- **Clean-room where required.** Components without clear licensing upstream (e.g. off-chain matching engines and client tooling) were **not** forked — the RFQ engine, feed poster, and maker bot are original implementations.
