# Start with the decision the holder already understands

The first Hedge product is a BTC covered call. It asks for three inputs:

1. How much BTCB do you want to cover?
2. What future price would make you comfortable giving up additional upside?
3. How long are you willing to make that commitment?

Those choices define a covered call. Market makers then compete to pay a premium for the upside above the selected price through the selected date.

## The exchange

The holder is not receiving free yield. The premium pays for a specific trade-off.

| At expiry | Economic outcome |
|---|---|
| BTC finishes below the target | The call expires without a payout. The holder keeps the covered BTCB and the premium, subject to protocol fees. |
| BTC finishes above the target | The holder keeps the premium but gives up the economic upside above the target on the covered amount through cash settlement. |

The BTCB remains in the holder's protocol account while the cash result reflects the option outcome. A cash shortfall can become borrowing against collateral, so the product must show the full obligation rather than describe the position as risk-free.

<Warning>
Premium is compensation for capped upside and settlement obligations. It is not guaranteed return, interest, or downside protection for the BTCB price.
</Warning>

## Why this is a useful wedge

The story is concrete enough for a first product and demanding enough to prove the underlying system:

- The holder must understand the outcome before signing.
- Makers must price a bespoke combination of amount, target, and date.
- The market must choose a quote fairly.
- The protocol must enforce collateral, fees, and settlement.

Solving that loop creates a foundation for other held-asset outcomes without asking the customer to think like a derivatives desk.
